## consonni2019wikilinkgraphs — WikiLinkGraphs: A Complete, Longitudinal and Multi-Language Dataset of the Wikipedia Link Networks

Consonni, Laniado, Montresor. ICWSM 2019 (vol. 13, pp. 598–607). arXiv 1902.04298 (cs.SI), v2 4 Apr 2019. Dataset on Zenodo (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.2539424), code at github.com/WikiLinkGraphs. Verified against the arXiv PDF (all numbers below are transcribed from its Tables 1, 3, 4, 5 and figures, not inferred unless flagged).

### What the paper actually does

This is a **dataset/resource paper**, not a modeling paper. It builds and releases the network of *editor-added main-text internal links* ("wikilinks") for the 9 largest Wikipedia language editions, as **yearly snapshots** (March 1 of each year, 2001→2018, 18 per language), plus supporting event-stream datasets.

**The central methodological claim — main-text wikilinks vs. the raw dump graph.** The pre-existing standard sources for the Wikipedia link graph are the MediaWiki API "Link" property and the `pagelinks` SQL table dump. Those include **transcluded links** — links generated automatically by templates (navboxes, infoboxes). A single navigation template like `{{Computer science}}` renders as a table of 146 links to related articles; inserting the template into an article is one edit but injects a whole clique of links, and template content changes independently of the pages that include it. Consonni et al. argue these inflate density and create "big cliques and other anomalous patterns" that do not reflect intentional editorial semantic judgments.

Their method: parse the **wikitext of every revision of every article** (namespace 0 only) from the full-history XML dumps, and extract links with a single regex over the `[[title|anchor]]` syntax:
```
\[\[ (?P<link>[^\n\|\]\[\<\>\{\}]{0,256}) (?: \| (?P<anchor>[^\[]*?) )? \]\]
```
Because templates in wikitext are `{{...}}` invocations, not `[[...]]`, template-generated links **never appear in the wikitext** and are automatically excluded. Section links (`#`) are split off a posteriori. This discards **more than half** of all links vs. the rendered/pagelinks graph — see Table 5 below — leaving "all and only the links intentionally added by editors."

**Pipeline stages** (their Fig. 2): RawWikiLinks (one row per link occurrence per revision, with page/revision/user/section metadata) → resolve redirects per-timestamp (a `#REDIRECT [[target]]` page is an "alternative title"; language-specific redirect keywords in their Table 2) → WikilinkSnapshots (per-revision `is_active` flag: did the target page exist at that moment) → WikiLinkGraphs (deduplicated directed edge list `page_from → page_to`; **multi-links between the same pair are collapsed to one edge** because Wikipedia policy says link once, at first mention).

**Redirect handling is a first-class concern** (they cite Hill & Shaw 2014 showing unresolved redirects bias prior research). Redirects are resolved to their target *as of each snapshot's timestamp*, following chains, **but the redirect pages themselves are retained as nodes** — each appears as a node with exactly one outgoing link and (usually) zero incoming links. This is why node counts vastly exceed article counts.

**Scale (concrete numbers).**
- Input processing (Table 1): ~40M article pages, >1B revisions across the 9 editions. English dump alone: 138 GB compressed, 13,750,758 pages, 543,746,894 revisions.
- Released dataset: 172 files, 142 GB total.
- **English (en) 2018-03-01 snapshot (Table 3): N = 13,685,337 nodes, E = 163,380,007 edges** → average out-degree ≈ **11.9**. (Node count ≫ the ~5.6M content articles because of retained redirect nodes.)
- Other 2018 editions for scale: de 3.59M N / 59.5M E; fr 3.44M N / 57.8M E; ru 3.36M N / 37.4M E; es 3.03M N / 38.3M E; it 2.12M N / 37.8M E. Average degree clusters around ~11–17 across editions.
- **Longitudinal growth (Tables 3–4, Fig. 3):** monotone near-linear growth of edges per edition after the first ~4 years; e.g. en goes 31 edges (2001) → 1.3M (2003) → 34.2M (2007) → 105M (2013) → 163M (2018). Swedish is the outlier with anomalous jumps attributed to bot mass-imports (Cebuano was excluded outright for the same reason).

**Pagelinks comparison (Table 5), the quantitative payoff of the "editor-added only" claim** — for English: pagelinks *all namespaces* = 1,117,233,757 links; pagelinks *ns0-only* = 476,959,671; **WikiLinkGraphs = 163,380,007**. So WLG is ~34% of the ns0 pagelinks graph and ~15% of the full pagelinks graph — i.e. templates/transclusions account for roughly two-thirds of the ns0 "link" mass that WLG strips out.

**Analyses shown** are illustrative use-cases, not deep topology characterization: (1) the pagelinks comparison above; (2) cross-language PageRank (igraph) over the 2018 snapshot — the *United States* article is #1 or #2 in 7 of 9 editions; Dutch and Swedish top-10s are dominated by biology taxa (a bot/linking-guideline artifact). They then list research opportunities: graph streaming, link recommendation, link addition/removal (unlink) prediction, anomaly/spurious-link detection, controversy mapping, cross-cultural comparison.

**Honest limitation for our purposes:** the paper does **not** report a degree-distribution power-law exponent, clustering coefficient, diameter, or component structure. Those quantities are well-established in the broader Wikipedia-graph literature (scale-free, heavy-tailed in-degree, small diameter) but are *not* measured in this paper. Any claim we make about hubs/power-law rests on the general literature plus the PageRank concentration they do show, not on a number in this paper — flagging so we don't miscite.

### Methodology: theirs vs. ours

This paper is the **provenance of our wiki edges**, so the comparison is about *edge construction*, not modeling. Our wiki pipeline lives in `data/wiki_graph_extractor/` (see code brief `data_pipelines.md`; source `extract.py`, `build_graph.py`, `normalization.py`).

**Shared intent — strip templates, keep editorial links.** Independently of WLG, our extractor makes the *same* core decision: `extract.py` runs `remove_templates` (deletes `{{...}}`) plus `remove_stub_templates` (navboxes/stubs that survive), then converts wikitext `[[title|label]]` → markdown `[label](target)`. So both pipelines target *main-text, editor-added* links and both exclude transclusion cliques. This is the single most important convergence: **the topology WLG characterizes (post-template, avg out-degree ~12 on full en) is qualitatively the graph family our edges are drawn from**, not the inflated pagelinks graph. Our measured enwiki numbers (`data_pipelines.md`: 275,199 nodes / 2,321,731 edges, avg degree 8.44) are far smaller than WLG's 13.7M/163M because **we operate on a single Cirrus-search JSON snapshot of current articles (rendered ns0), heavily subsetted, not the full revision history**, and we drop redirect nodes and out-of-corpus targets — but the *per-node linking behavior* (~8–12 out-links) is in the same regime WLG reports.

**Where we diverge — five concrete axes:**

1. **Snapshot vs. longitudinal.** WLG's headline contribution is 18 time-resolved snapshots with per-link creation/removal timestamps (`is_active`, RawWikiLinks event stream). **We use a single static snapshot.** We throw away the entire temporal dimension. Their `RawWikiLinks`/link-addition-removal stream is exactly the substrate for "when was this edge added" questions we never ask.

2. **Redirect resolution.** WLG resolves redirects *per-timestamp* following chains, and keeps redirect pages as one-out-link nodes. Our pipeline has **no timestamp** (single snapshot) and, per `data_pipelines.md` gotcha #6, `fix_mediawiki_links` *invents* `md5[:6]` link ids that will **not** match `normalize_identifier`, guaranteeing danglers — i.e. our redirect/alias handling is weaker and lossy where theirs is a validated first-class step.

3. **Link resolution rate — measured vs. unmeasured.** WLG explicitly quantifies its filtering (Table 5: WLG is 34% of ns0 pagelinks) and flags every link `is_active` against page existence at that time. **Our resolution fraction is NOT logged** (`data_pipelines.md` gotcha #6: "Wiki resolution rate UNMEASURED"). Our edge exists *iff* the normalized target is in our node set; outgoing danglers are kept as "latent cross-source", incoming links only if in-set. We have no analogue to their `is_active` audit, so we cannot state what fraction of `[[...]]` become realized edges.

4. **Target identity — raw title vs. resolved page_id.** WLG resolves to a MediaWiki `page_id` (redirect-collapsed), giving canonical node identity. Ours is text-mediated and asymmetric (`normalization.py`): the edge *target written in text* is a RAW spaces-preserved title, matched by `MarkdownLinkDetector` against `raw_identifier`; the internal graph key is the *hashed* `{normalized_body}_{md5[:6](raw)}`. This asymmetry is deliberate (the runtime link detector must re-find link positions from tokens, positions are never stored) but it means our node identity is a normalization convention, not MediaWiki's authoritative id — a source of silent mismatches (cf. MEMORY "stack graph stale normalization").

5. **Multi-edge dedup.** WLG collapses repeated same-pair links to a single edge. Our `GraphIndex` (traversal.py brief) does **no adjacency dedup** — multiplicity weights the random walk. So a page that links the same target twice biases our traversal sampler where WLG would see one edge.

**Axis summary for the paper's framing:** WLG is a *data-construction* reference — it neither trains on structure nor retrieves at inference; it produces the edge list. Our contribution is the modeling layer *on top of* an edge list of exactly this kind (graph-traversal packing + a causal cross-doc attention grant, used in pretraining and inference alike). WLG's relevance is that it (a) legitimizes the "editor-added main-text link" as the semantically meaningful edge — the same edge we bet on — and (b) supplies the topology (scale-free hubs, ~12 avg degree, redirect noise) whose consequences our packing/masking must survive.

### Predictions & open questions for our method

The topology WLG documents lets us predict behavior of our BFS/DFS packing (`traversal.py`) and cross-doc attention grants (`cross_doc_mask.py`):

- **Hubs will dominate BFS frontiers and blow up grant density.** WLG's PageRank result (US/country/language hubs sit orders of magnitude above the median; taxa hubs in nl/sv) plus the general scale-free structure means a small set of nodes carry enormous in-degree. Our **bidirectional BFS** (used both for `val_community` packs in `split_graph.py` and as a live growth strategy) expands `neighbors_out + neighbors_in`; hitting one hub pulls in a huge, low-specificity neighborhood in a single hop. Prediction: BFS packs seeded near hubs will be **dense but semantically diffuse** (a pack full of "linked from United States"), inflating realized grant count toward `max_grants` and possibly saturating the mask. DFS (LIFO + shuffled neighbors) will instead produce **long thin chains** that under-use hubs — more coherent topically but lower grant density per pack. This directly predicts the BFS-vs-DFS ablation should trade grant *density* against grant *specificity*.

- **Grant density is capped well below raw degree — quantify it.** WLG avg out-degree ~12 sounds like ~12 grants per doc, but three of our mechanisms cut it: (i) out-of-index neighbors silently dropped (`dataset.py:179-181`), worse for us because we operate on a small subset of the full en graph, so most of a hub's 12+ targets are *not in our corpus*; (ii) the DAG gate (`cross_doc_mask.py:417-423`, target must start before the link) drops grants unless `prefer_targets_first` topo-ordering placed the target earlier; (iii) `max_grants` cap. Prediction: **realized grants per pack ≪ WLG's nominal degree**, and the dominant loss is corpus-subsetting, not the mask. We should measure realized-vs-raw density (a `traversal.py`-brief open action) and expect the gap to widen the more we subset.

- **Heavy-tailed degree ⇒ heavy-tailed pack composition ⇒ variance in the training signal.** Because degree is heavy-tailed, uniform-seeded packing (`pack_sampler.py` seeds uniformly regardless of strategy) will occasionally land on a hub and produce a wildly denser-than-average pack. Prediction: **high variance in cross-doc-attention supervision per step**; the compute-control concat baselines will see the same token budget but the linking-mask arm's *effective* edge signal will be spiky. Worth logging grants-per-pack distribution, not just the mean.

- **Redirect/alias noise predicts a floor of dead links.** WLG kept redirects as one-out-link orphan nodes and had to resolve them carefully; our pipeline's invented-md5 redirect ids (gotcha #6) guarantee a population of danglers. Prediction: a nontrivial fraction of `[[...]]` in our wiki text will fail to resolve to an in-corpus node and thus produce **no grant** — the model sees a `[label](target)` surface form with nothing fetched. If that fraction is large, the wiki arm's link signal is diluted and the linking inductive bias could look weaker than on cleaner sources (Python imports, arXiv cites). This makes *measuring* our resolution rate (WLG-style) a prerequisite for interpreting a weak wiki result.

- **What our design could resolve for them, and vice versa.** WLG lists "link recommendation" and "link addition prediction" as open opportunities but ships only a static edge list + PageRank. Our generative setup (a decoder that, at inference, *emits* a `[label](target)` link and fetches the target) is effectively a **generative link-prediction/recommendation model over exactly their graph** — a concrete instantiation of their proposed use case. Conversely, their **longitudinal** stream answers a question we cannot: whether edges we treat as ground truth are stable or ephemeral (added-then-removed). If our model is graded on predicting links, WLG's temporal data would let us *decontaminate* against edges that only existed transiently — an evaluation refinement their dataset uniquely enables.

### Gotchas

- **Node count ≠ article count.** WLG's 13.7M en "nodes" include ~8M redirect orphans. If we ever benchmark our graph scale against "WikiLinkGraphs has 13.7M nodes," we would be comparing our article-only, redirect-dropped node set against their redirect-inflated one — an apples-to-oranges factor of ~2.4×. Compare *content-article* counts, or explicitly note redirect inclusion.
- **The "avg degree ~12" is post-dedup and full-history-resolved.** Our per-snapshot, subset, non-deduped, unresolved-redirect number is not directly comparable. Don't cite WLG degree as a target for our realized density.
- **Template exclusion is a *modeling assumption*, not obviously correct.** WLG themselves note (their pagelinks discussion) that for *navigational* tasks the template links matter and should be included. Our pipeline inherits their exclusion. If a downstream eval rewards navigational reachability (e.g. Wikispeedia-style pathfinding), template links we (and they) dropped are exactly the edges that make the graph navigable — our sparse "editor-added only" graph could look artificially disconnected. Their choice is right for *semantic* edges, possibly wrong for *reachability* evals.
- **Redirect chains and per-timestamp resolution are non-trivial** — WLG treats this as a headline engineering challenge with language-specific keyword tables. Our single-snapshot, invented-id shortcut is exactly the corner they warn is a known source of research bias (Hill & Shaw 2014). Expect a measurable dangling-link floor.
- **Swedish/Cebuano bot inflation.** WLG excluded Cebuano and flagged Swedish as bot-inflated. If we ever go multilingual, blindly ingesting the "largest" editions imports bot-generated pseudo-structure (their sv PageRank top-10 is all biology taxa auto-linked by a template/bot). The size of an edition is not a proxy for the quality of its editorial link signal.
- **Regex extraction is shallow.** WLG's extractor is a single regex over `[[...]]` (they say so). It does not parse nested templates that emit `[[...]]` internally, piped-file syntax edge cases, etc. Our extractor is more elaborate (balanced-bracket File:/Image: handling, IPA-link special cases in `extract.py`) — but neither is a full wikitext parser, so both carry a residual extraction-error rate that neither paper bounds.
