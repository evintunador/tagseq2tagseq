\subsection{Cross-document attention beats flat concatenation}
On the Wikipedia HotpotQA cross-document benchmark, the link-aware \crossdoc model attains
a cross-document negative log-likelihood of \val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.nll_crossdoc}
against \val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.nll_flat} for the flat-linked baseline over the same
$n=\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.n}$ query--target pairs, a reduction of
$\Delta\mathrm{nll}=\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.delta_nll}$. The effect carries over to code:
on Java RepoBench cross-document completion, cross-document attention lowers NLL across all
traversals --- from \val{crossdoc.repobench_java.random.delta_nll} (random packing) to
\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.rw.delta_nll} (random-walk), with
\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.delta_nll} (bfs) and \val{crossdoc.repobench_java.dfs.delta_nll}
(dfs) in between --- and Python RepoBench cross-doc shows a $\Delta$nll of
\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.delta_nll} (max\_grants=256).

\subsection{The cross-document gain is specific, not memorization}
Two controls confirm the reduction above comes from reading the \emph{correct} linked
document, not from generic extra context or verbatim re-exposure of overlapping text.
First, a derangement \emph{placebo}: for each query we keep the original link identifiers
(so the grant still fires) but replace the granted document's content with another
example's, so the model attends to a wrong-but-plausible document. This collapses most of
the benefit, leaving a placebo separation (placebo minus cross-document NLL) of
\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.placebo_sep} (95\% CI
\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.placebo_sep.ci}) on HotpotQA, and
\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.placebo_sep}
(\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.placebo_sep.ci}) and
\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.placebo_sep}
(\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.placebo_sep.ci}) on Python and Java RepoBench --- all
CIs excluding zero. Second, we stratify the cross-document $\Delta$nll by the
target--context $n$-gram overlap $\alpha$ (RETRO's leakage protocol): even in the
lowest-overlap stratum ($\alpha<0.05$), where the target cannot be copied from the granted
context, the reduction persists at
\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll}
(\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll.ci},
$n=\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.leakage_lowalpha.n}$) on HotpotQA,
\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll}
(\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll.ci},
$n=\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.leakage_lowalpha.n}$) on Python, and
\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll}
(\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll.ci},
$n=\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.leakage_lowalpha.n}$) on Java --- again all CIs
excluding zero. The gain therefore reflects cross-document reasoning over the right link,
not surface duplication.

\subsection{Single-document benchmarks are unaffected}
Across the single-document controls (HellaSwag, ARC-easy, PIQA, WinoGrande, OpenBookQA,
BoolQ), the four masks are statistically indistinguishable. On HellaSwag the matched
wiki\_merged 4-mask set scores \val{singledoc.hellaswag.doc_causal} (\doccausal),
\val{singledoc.hellaswag.cross_doc_link} (\crossdoc),
\val{singledoc.hellaswag.doc_concatenated} (\concat), and
\val{singledoc.hellaswag.doc_concat_link} (\concatlink), with overlapping 95\% confidence
intervals (e.g.\ \val{singledoc.hellaswag.doc_causal.ci} for \doccausal vs
\val{singledoc.hellaswag.cross_doc_link.ci} for \crossdoc) --- cross-document attention has
nothing to attend to on isolated documents, as predicted. These models are undertrained
(accuracies near chance), so the claim is that the mask does not \emph{degrade}
single-document capability, not a strong-capability equivalence.

\subsection{Compute controls}
Granting the baselines more within-context attention does not close the gap. On code
RepoBench perplexity, \crossdoc reaches \val{compute.repobench_ppl.cross_doc_link}, whereas
\concatlink and \concat --- which see the same concatenated multi-document context --- sit at
\val{compute.repobench_ppl.doc_concat_link} and \val{compute.repobench_ppl.doc_concatenated}
respectively, and the plain \doccausal baseline at \val{compute.repobench_ppl.doc_causal}.
The compute controls remain well above \crossdoc, so the gain comes from the linking
inductive bias, not from additional attention FLOPs.

\subsection{Traversal ablation}
Graph-structured packing matters for the base language model independent of the
cross-document objective: final validation loss is \val{traversal.wiki_val_loss.graph}
under graph (bfs/dfs) packing versus \val{traversal.wiki_val_loss.random} under random
packing. The incremental cross-document benefit is itself traversal-robust --- the Java
cross-document $\Delta$nll above stays positive across all four bfs/dfs/random-walk/random
traversals --- so packing quality and cross-document attention are two decoupled axes.

\subsection{Graph-sparsity scaling law}
\label{sec:sparsity}
We interpolate the fraction of resolved links a model sees, from $0\%$ (\doccausal)
to $100\%$ (\crossdoc), by seeded per-edge subsampling of the grant map, and measure
the within-model cross-doc benefit $\Delta\mathrm{nll} =
\mathrm{nll}(\doccausal)-\mathrm{nll}(\crossdoc)$ on each dataset's
\texttt{val\_community} split. We do this both at eval time (fix a trained model, vary
the links it attends to; \cref{fig:sparsity-eval}) and at train time (retrain on the
reduced graph; \cref{fig:sparsity-train}), and cross the two axes into a surface
(\cref{fig:sparsity-grid}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\cref{fig:sparsity-eval} (eval-time lines + cross-dataset law).} How a
  fixed trained model's cross-doc benefit grows as it is allowed to attend to more of
  the graph's links. \emph{Meaning:} for code the benefit rises near-linearly with
  effective link density and the cross-dataset fit is strong ($r{=}\val{sparsity.crossdoc_law.eval_r_code}$), so it
  extrapolates \emph{above} today's densest corpus; the honest density axis is
  effective co-packed grants/pack, not raw out-degree. Text (wiki, arxiv) is flat or
  slightly negative.
  \item \textbf{\cref{fig:sparsity-train} (train-time lines).} The same sweep but
  \emph{retraining} at each density, so the model learns under the sparsity it is
  given. \emph{Meaning:} the code curves are monotone but strongly concave --- most of
  the benefit is captured by the first ${\sim}25\%$ of links (diminishing returns to
  training density) --- and sit at or above the eval-time curve at low density.
  \item \textbf{\cref{fig:sparsity-grid} (2D train\,$\times$\,eval surface).} The
  headline result: the dominant gradient is \emph{horizontal} (eval keep), not vertical
  (train keep). \emph{Meaning:} the cross-doc payoff is overwhelmingly an
  \emph{inference-time} capability --- a \doccausal-trained code model (bottom row)
  exploits cross-doc links at test time as well as, or better than, one trained with
  them, so having linked content in context at inference matters far more than
  training-time exposure. Wiki is the lone net-negative (blue) panel, and there
  \emph{training} on cross-doc links makes it worse while a \doccausal wiki model is
  barely affected.
\end{itemize}
