\subsection{Cross-document attention beats scoring without the target}
\label{sec:results:crossdoc}
On the Wikipedia HotpotQA cross-document benchmark, the \crossdoc model (bfs) attains a
cross-document negative log-likelihood of \val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.nll_crossdoc}
against \val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.nll_flat} for the same answers scored flat, over
$n=\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.n}$ fired query--target pairs: a reduction of
$\Delta\mathrm{nll}=\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.delta_nll}$ nats per token. The effect
carries over to code, where dependencies are more local and the margins accordingly
smaller: on Java RepoBench cross-document completion, cross-document attention lowers
NLL under every traversal --- from \val{crossdoc.repobench_java.random.delta_nll}
(random packing) to \val{crossdoc.repobench_java.rw.delta_nll} (random walk) --- and
Python RepoBench cross-doc shows
$\Delta\mathrm{nll}=\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.delta_nll}$
($n=\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.n}$). \Cref{tab:traversal} gives every arm.

\subsection{The cross-document gain is specific, not memorization}
\label{sec:results:specificity}
Two controls confirm the reduction above comes from reading the \emph{correct} linked
document, not from generic extra context or verbatim re-exposure of overlapping text.
First, a derangement \emph{placebo}: for each query we keep the original link identifiers
(so the grant still fires) but replace the granted document's content with another
example's, so the model attends to a wrong-but-plausible document. This collapses most of
the benefit, leaving a placebo separation (placebo minus cross-document NLL) of
\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.placebo_sep} (95\% CI
\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.placebo_sep.ci}) on HotpotQA, and
\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.placebo_sep}
(\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.placebo_sep.ci}) and
\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.placebo_sep}
(\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.placebo_sep.ci}) on Python and Java RepoBench --- all
CIs excluding zero. Second, we stratify the cross-document $\Delta$nll by the
target--context $n$-gram overlap $\alpha$ (RETRO's leakage protocol): even in the
lowest-overlap stratum ($\alpha<0.05$), where the target cannot be copied from the granted
context, the reduction persists at
\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll}
(\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll.ci},
$n=\val{crossdoc.wiki_hotpotqa.leakage_lowalpha.n}$) on HotpotQA,
\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll}
(\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll.ci},
$n=\val{crossdoc.repobench_python.leakage_lowalpha.n}$) on Python, and
\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll}
(\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.leakage_lowalpha.delta_nll.ci},
$n=\val{crossdoc.repobench_java.bfs.leakage_lowalpha.n}$) on Java --- again all CIs
excluding zero. The gain therefore reflects cross-document reasoning over the right link,
not surface duplication.

\subsection{Single-document benchmarks are unaffected}
\label{sec:results:singledoc}
Cross-document attention has nothing to attend to on an isolated document, and the
controls confirm it does not change the base model (\Cref{tab:singledoc-tuned}): at the
tuned recipe the three recipe-matched masks are indistinguishable on isolated-document
held-out perplexity and on the two benchmarks these models clear chance on, HellaSwag
and ARC-easy, with overlapping $95\%$ intervals. The full four-mask panel (HellaSwag,
ARC-easy, PIQA, WinoGrande, OpenBookQA, BoolQ) on an earlier, undertrained matched set
tells the same story at chance-level accuracy (\Cref{tab:singledoc-4mask},
\Cref{app:results}). The claim is that the mask does not \emph{degrade}
single-document capability, not a strong-capability equivalence: these are small models
trained for a few billion tokens.

\subsection{Compute controls}
\label{sec:results:controls}
Granting the baselines more within-context attention does not close the gap
(\Cref{tab:compute-controls}). On code RepoBench perplexity, \crossdoc reaches
\val{compute.repobench_ppl.cross_doc_link}, whereas \concatlink and \concat --- which
see the same concatenated multi-document context and strictly more attention --- sit at
\val{compute.repobench_ppl.doc_concat_link} and \val{compute.repobench_ppl.doc_concatenated}
respectively, and the plain \doccausal baseline at \val{compute.repobench_ppl.doc_causal}.
The residual that isolates the contribution is \crossdoc against \concatlink at
identical connectivity, and it favours the link-gated mask; \concat, the most
permissive condition, is the \emph{worst} of the four, consistent with the packing
literature's finding that indiscriminate cross-document attention hurts. The matching
contrast on the Wikipedia HotpotQA benchmark --- \concatlink scored cross-doc against
\crossdoc --- is \fillin{$\Delta$nll, wiki concat-link arm}.

\subsection{Traversal ablation}
\label{sec:results:traversal}
Graph-structured packing matters for the base language model independent of the
cross-document objective: final validation loss on Wikipedia is
\val{traversal.wiki_val_loss.graph} under graph (bfs/dfs) packing versus
\val{traversal.wiki_val_loss.random} under random packing. The incremental
cross-document benefit is itself traversal-robust --- every Wikipedia and Java arm of
\Cref{tab:traversal} is positive, with random packing the weakest --- so packing
quality and cross-document attention are two decoupled axes: traversal strongly moves
base-model loss and only weakly moves the within-model cross-document gain.

\subsection{The cross-document benefit scales with realized link density}
\label{sec:results:density}\label{sec:sparsity}
With corpus, packing, and model held fixed and only the fraction $\kappa$ of resolved
grants varied, the community-pack $\Delta\mathrm{nll}$ on every code graph rises
monotonically with $\kappa$ at evaluation time, near-linearly
(\Cref{fig:sparsity-eval}, panel A), and is pinned to zero at
$\kappa=0$ (the \doccausal identity; the instrument was verified to reproduce
$\Delta=0$ exactly on real checkpoints). Which edges are kept does not matter, only how
many: subsampling by edge and by node give the same lines. Pooling the nine code graphs
and regressing $\Delta\mathrm{nll}$ on the number of grants the mask actually realizes
per pack (out-degree $\times$ co-packing rate $\times\kappa$) yields a single
cross-dataset line with correlation $r=\val{sparsity.crossdoc_law.eval_r_code}$ over
\fillin{$n$} points and slope \fillin{nats per realized grant}
(\Cref{fig:sparsity-eval}, panel B; pooled regression detail in
\Cref{app:sparsity-regression}), extrapolating above today's densest corpus. Raw graph
out-degree is the wrong axis, with correlation \fillin{$r$, out-degree}: it counts
edges whose targets are never co-packed and hence never attended.

Retraining at each $\kappa$ and re-evaluating at full density gives the train-time
line (\Cref{fig:sparsity-train}), which is monotone but strongly \emph{concave}: most
of the benefit is already present when the model trained on a quarter of the edges,
and the train-time curve lies above the eval-time curve at low $\kappa$ (training on a
sparse graph beats evaluating a dense-trained model at that sparsity). The
two-dimensional train-$\kappa\times$eval-$\kappa$ grid (\Cref{fig:sparsity-grid})
makes the asymmetry explicit: the dominant gradient is horizontal --- moving along the
eval-density axis swings $\Delta$ by \fillin{eval-axis swing} while moving along the
train-density axis moves it by only \fillin{train-axis swing}. The limiting case is
the $\kappa_\text{train}=0$ row --- a \doccausal-trained model evaluated under the
\crossdoc mask --- which on TypeScript recovers $\Delta\mathrm{nll}=$
\fillin{dc-trained TS} against \fillin{cdl-trained TS} for the model trained with
grants. On code, then, the cross-document benefit is overwhelmingly an
\emph{inference-time} effect: what the mask exposes at evaluation matters far more than
whether the model saw grants during training. The instrument itself is validated by a
traversal-time spot-check --- really removing edges from the graph before packing, so
co-packing changes too, lands on the same dose--response curve at matched effective
density (\Cref{app:sparsity-traversal}). The Wikipedia graph is excluded from this
analysis pending an audit of the community-pack instrument on that corpus
(\Cref{sec:analysis}).

\subsection{Density-aware scheduling efficiency}
\label{sec:results:efficiency}
Precomputing packs and scheduling by density buys wall-clock time twice: link
detection leaves the training step, and no rank stalls at the all-reduce waiting for a
denser pack. Measured on a two-node, two-GPUs-per-node run on The Stack at $32$k
context (\Cref{tab:efficiency}), mean step time drops from
\val{efficiency.step_time.live_mean}\,s to \val{efficiency.step_time.precomputed_mean}\,s
(a \val{efficiency.speedup_mean}$\times$ speedup) and worst-case step time from
\val{efficiency.step_time.live_max}\,s to \val{efficiency.step_time.precomputed_max}\,s
(\val{efficiency.speedup_max}$\times$). The live path's step-time distribution is broad
and structureless (coefficient of variation \val{efficiency.cov.live}\%), while under
density bucketing the within-bucket variation collapses to a mean of
\val{efficiency.cov.precomputed_within_bucket}\% --- the remaining spread is
\emph{between} buckets, deterministic and density-driven (\Cref{fig:density-timing}).
The rank-stall component is most visible across nodes and near zero on a single
NVLink node.
